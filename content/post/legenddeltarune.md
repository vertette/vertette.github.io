(This article will spoil *Deltarune* up to Chapter 5.)

There's a fantastic legend in the gaming industry that goes something like this: back in 2011, an aspiring composer and musician was suffering from an intense fever. When he went to sleep, he dreamt about seeing the highly emotional ending of a game that didn't exist, and once he woke up, he was determined to make that game a reality. Of course that developer was Toby Fox, the future developer of *Undertale*, a wildly successful and influential indie game that everyone with even a passing interest in games has heard of. He got a lot of help from Kickstarter backers and team members to make that game a reality, but he's still known as *the* person behind the game, and for good reason. 

While I had my gripes with the game, it can't be denied it was almost entirely his brainchild. His writing and design style can be felt throughout most of the game, with a focus on simple but memorable and expressive characters, irreverent internet and/or anime humor, playing around with medium and genre conventions, constant references to his favorite JRPGs like *Mother* and *Brandish* (especially *Brandish*) and the occasional moments dipping into surreal horror. This style can be seen in the Halloween Hack as well, although much less... refined.

His actual dream game would only come later, released in waves of chapters due to its much bigger scope: *Deltarune*. This was the game that Toby saw in his dreams, with *Undertale* now retroactively acting as a warm-up round of sorts for the "real thing". And when fans got their hands on the first four chapters, it's easy to see why most thought it was proving to be an improvement: a better presentation, an improved combat system, a much more interesting alternate route and even addressing some common *Undertale* complaints like the lack of a run button. Its mystery-heavy narrative and a few ARGs helped to keep people talking between chapter releases, with many clues and red herrings spread throughout the narrative keeping fans guessing. 

That's why it's so disappointing that, after all this build-up, Chapter 5 barely even feels like Toby's work. Obviously he would need to increase the size of his team in order to get this game done in his lifetime, as no dog is an island. But having finished the most recent chapter, it feels like I played a fangame rather than an official release. That isn't saying it's bad, necessarily — I've enjoyed my fair share of fangames I would happily recommend to anyone — but it feels different in ways that are sometimes obvious, and sometimes harder to place, which makes one wonder exactly how much Toby was even involved with the creation of this chapter. 

It was somewhere during my third or fourth platforming section that I realized I hadn't had a turn based fight in a while. Granted, those platforming sections are fun enough and *Deltarune*'s RPG mechanics have never been its strong point, but by now it feels like the game is both painfully aware of how hard it's stretched the limits of its core mechanics and how hard it's trying to jangle keys in the audience's face to prevent players from noticing, with very gimmicky fights (the court battle against Blue and Yellow working as a mix of a fight and simplified Ace Attorney case being a particularly good example) or elaborate sections that turn the game into something else entirely. In Chapter 3 this felt like a fun surprise that fit the theme of the chapter, but the more the game employs these kinds of tricks, the more they feel like an expensive crutch. 

Considering how long Chapter 3 was stuck in development hell due to how different it was from earlier chapters and how the team had to keep tweaking the pacing so it wouldn't feel like a slog (and a lot of people complained it felt like a slog anyway), it's bizarre how the team would make the exact same mistake twice. Whoever's idea it was to turn half of the chapter into a side-scrolling platformer with its own mechanics, gimmicks and fights, they're making design decisions that don't make good use of the game's core mechanics and are also incredibly resource-intensive and time-consuming to implement. It feels like the worst possible use of a team that I'm sure is already trying its best to finish the game before the ten-year anniversary of the first demo's release date passes.

![A screenshot of Deltarune Chapter 5, where Kris, Susie and Ralsei are jumping through cliffs](/img/deltarune_cliffs.png)
*Good old Deltarune, the RPG famous for its engaging platforming and elaborate Dark World environments.*

Those platforming sections are incredibly impressive, to be sure. The way the map animates during the switch from top-down to side-view never gets old, and the game employs fantastic animations, elaborate environments and even dramatic lighting to striking effect, which is incredibly hard to do in a pixelated game, but only makes it incredibly obvious just how out of place it all feels. The Dark Worlds have always employed a lot of dark colors and negative space as a contrast to the "real" world of *Deltarune*, but the cliff sections in particular look nothing like a Dark World, compared to the elegant and solemn gardens and even the somewhat fantastical Flower Castle. Those environments also feel out of place in a different way though, as this chapter's Dark World just can't seem to make up its mind what it's actually themed about. 

The previous Dark Worlds were always very easy to understand; a Dark World made in an unused classroom where they store all the old junk, with environments and enemy designs based around games, with chess and playing cards patterns. A Dark World made in a computer lab, with digital cities full of ads where you encounter spambots, cats and a monarch that talks like a Twitter feed. Chapter 5 seems to be about nostalgia at first, both for *Undertale* and *Deltarune*'s first chapter, with a a lot of plants thrown into the mix because it was made in Asgore's flower shop. So far this make sense for a character who is stuck in the past and sells flowers, but then it throws in a few Japanese elements like torii gates until you're platforming in a distinctly Japanese castle complete with enemies that scream [YOOOO](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VKMw2it8dQY), sliding door puzzles and an obligatory bath scene, at which point the plant motif might as well not exist. 

This lack of committing to a theme is reflected in the designs of the Plantners: only Flowery, Aqua and Pink actually fit the Japanese theme while the other characters look like designs all pulled from completely separate works. A divorced man like Asgore seeking refuge in fan-servicey media isn't even a huge stretch, and would also handily explain why Flowery is designed to feel like the perfect boyfriend out of a poorly translated dating sim (complete with bit-crushed and nonsensical voice lines), but the chapter never puts this kind of thought into the theme when the game always did before, even though it'd be easy to just throw a few anime DVDs or games around the place. There's a single manga on top of the TV (which was a "superhero comic" in previous chapters) and a throwaway line where Asgore mentions getting the doll that later becomes Pink as a gift, and that's it.

![A screenshot of Deltarune Chapter 5, showing all the Plantners' designs](/img/deltarune_flowers.png)
*From left to right, it's green trapezoids, purple Illusion of Gaia NPC, cyan little girl, golden Japanese VN man, orange mouse, yellow Muppet cowboy and blue Steven Universe gem. Whatever words you might use to describe the designs of the Plantners, you wouldn't use "coherent".*

But all of those gripes are of course peanuts compared to the actual quality of the writing, which I want to highlight because it feels like it's all over the place. As the Dark World takes place in the Flower King, you'd think this chapter would be about Asgore, and yet the main characters only interact with him *three times total*. The first time Flowery conveniently throws him off-screen, the second time he angrily orders Kris and Susie to leave the Dark World, and the last time he's had his character arc *entirely off-screen* where he decides that oh, I guess I don't care about my previous motivation anymore. Then he has a speech about his backstory that's so frustratingly vague, "Asgore the Vagueposting King" quickly became a fandom meme. 

We get other scenes where he interacts with Flowery, but after the main characters get to Flower Castle, they mostly involve Asgore being angry at things. For a man who's always been portrayed as a patient man and doting father, it feels hard to buy that Asgore, out all characters, would just toss his child out of his store and tell them "don't come back" without any further explanation, no matter how much danger he thought they were in. 

I'm sure that was the point, to show how desperate the man has become for answers, but the potential in this character point isn't used well. There's one good moment of dramatic irony where Asgore proclaims he will parade the corpse of whomever is opening all the Dark Fountains around town while Kris is off-screen, but it doesn't feel as dramatic as it could've been if the Fun Gang actually interacted with Asgore throughout the chapter like they did with Noelle or Gerson. You end up feeling like a game just told you Asgore had an arc without actually getting to see it, which feels like a recurring problem in this chapter.

Ralsei and Susie also suffer from this problem. Ralsei's arc in Chapter 5 is based around Flowery, the leader of the Plantners who tries to give Ralsei advice, but the way he comes across as incredibly smug and condescending and the ways his philosophy differs from Ralsei causes them to clash. 

That could, and does, lead to some interesting arguments between the two, but the way it's built up is lacking, in the sense it lacks any build up at all. Ralsei has always been shown to be a very meek and spineless person who puts up with *a lot* throughout the story, and yet getting shoved into a closet instantly sours him on Flowery? If the arc of becoming his own person involved him expressing his anger much more often as he gradually gets to know and hate Flowery, that'd be one thing, but for someone who has literally volunteered to let people step on him, it feels so out-of-character that even Susie points it out. (Writing hint: having people point out the flaws in your writing doesn't mean it's not a flaw.) 

Later in the chapter, there's a cutscene between Ralsei and Flowery where the former claims he could burn the latter if he wanted to, which seems like an appropiately dramatic time for a demonstration, but instead the reveal is wasted on this bizarre throwaway moment where Ralsei demonstrates that he can use fire magic to save Susie from a disposable level obstacle. It's been hinted before that he can use fire, so you'd think this would be a dramatic plot point, but there is no build up, no weight and it isn't referred to again. "Ralsei can use fire", OK cool. And after Flowery sacrifices himself to try and save Asgore, Ralsei suddenly cares for him after all those scenes where he was this close to burning Flowery to a crisp? He's so upset he stays behind with the corpse instead of coming along to watch the Fountain get sealed, even.

In contrast, after Susie's incredibly traumatic day of going through the Dark World, making friends with someone who turned out to be dead all along, getting her ass kicked around by the Knight and even defeating a Titan, all in a futile attempt to save someone who it turned out wasn't ever actually in danger, her arc starts with her happily preparing for a date at the festival. It's such a bizarre turn of events that I've seen people defend it by claiming the whiplash is intentional, to show Susie is either coping by embracing the Prophecy or how she's being influenced by the Prophecy. 

That would be an interesting direction to take her character if the game actually showed this was meant to be the case, for example by having Ralsei or Lancer point out she's acting differently, but nobody ever reacts to her behavior like that's the case so it's too ambiguos to say what it's trying to do with this. That ambiguity also means I can't tell what the game is trying to say about Susie and Noelle's romance. Noelle's crush was always portrayed as being based on an idealization of Susie, influenced by more than a few hormones and possibly even a hole in her heart that Dess left, but we don't really see enough of them together to get a good sense of how their relationship will play out, because it conveniently skips over the scene where they actually start bonding at the lake. The game just shows us the aftermath where Susie tells us she had the "best day ever" with Noelle, and that's apparently meant to be enough? 

From there, the vast majority of Susie's dialogue throughout the chapter seems to be about Noelle. At the end she even describes the Dark Worlds as Kris and Susie's special thing only the two of them have, only to immediately backtrack and claim Noelle needs to be involved as well and even starts hanging up decorations as her best friend walks home alone after seeing their father get kidnapped. Is this meant to show Susie's coping in an unhealthy way? That she's being a neglectful friend who needs to learn how to maintain friendships and relationships? That the Prophecy is influencing her behavior? If someone could just *respond* to her behavior, we'd have some insight into what the chapter is trying to say.

![A screenshot of Deltarune Chapter 5, showing the boss fight against Flowery](/img/deltarune_flowery.png)
*You go out of your way to write lyrics for your character's fight song who uses voice clips regularly, and he doesn't sing it with bit-crushed vocals, Tales of Phantasia style? Those Hatsune Miku voicebanks must have been more expensive than I thought.*

As for the actual events of the chapter, there's the Festival followed by the Dark World. After all that build up from previous chapters it felt like the Festival would be important to the plot, particularly after the stakes were raised so high in Chapter 4, but story-wise not much really happens. It's not even an intentional anti-climax for the sake of bathos as Toby likes to do, it's just an ordinary festival with snacks and games, only one of which is playable (and only when you pick Kris and Noelle). It's got a few nice character beats — I like the conversation between Kris and Noelle on the ferris wheel, which is hidden away for some reason — but it's rushed through so we can get to the date between Susie and Noelle. 

The bonding between Susie and Noelle similarly gets rushed through so that Kris and Susie can enter another Dark World, and then we do the typical *Deltarune* thing of meeting some kooky characters we fight a few times up until the end where the Knight shows up to kidnap Asgore. We do find another shelter code, but it happens so incidentally that it took me out of the moment. What the story was moving towards, finding the shelter codes to rescue the kidnapped people of Hometown, only moves forward a tiny bit at the end of the chapter, and what's even more bizarre is that it would've only taken a few lines to fix this while making the Fun Gang actually take actions to move the plot forward, almost like the main characters in a story: "Wait, Kris, wasn't your dad chief of police? Let's ask him if he knows anything about the shelter codes! Oh no, there's a Dark World in your dad's shop!" 

It's such a bizarre way to pace a single story when Chapter 3 was already meant to be the final fun adventure before things get serious when the Knight shows up, kicks your ass and kidnaps Undyne. Chapter 4 followed up on that momentum with *Deltarune* at its most dramatic, and to me at its most interesting, and for Chapter 5 to slow down the pacing again for another "one last fun adventure" feels tedious when the story just got going. And this isn't even getting into the fact that Chapter 3 had actual story and thematic reasons for being paced the way it was, between Tenna deliberately stalling the Fun Gang to keep Toriel locked up and TV being a dying medium coasting on inertia and reruns for the past two decades now. Chapter 5, on the other hand, feels like the premise of the story was an afterthought.

Similarly, Asgore's kidnapping is also treated as an afterthought, though in this case it seems like an unfortunate victim of the game needing to juggle all these other plot points like Flowery's fight with the Knight and his sacrifice, the Fountain getting sealed, finding the shelter code, walking Susie to the school and her welcoming Noelle to the Dark World, but it does mean the chapter about Asgore can barely remember him. There's no real sense of urgency or gravitas like when Undyne was kidnapped, who is someone that Kris and Susie barely even knew. Ralsei is too busy grieving the death of someone he hated only a half an hour ago to react to the kidnapping, Susie is too busy hanging up cute decorations around Castletown for her girlfriend, and Kris is too much of a main character to respond to much of anything in this chapter. The way Chapter 4 ended was great, contrasting the beaten down and traumatized Kris and Susie finding Toriel and Sans acting like drunk idiots making them feel even worse. 

In contrast, I don't know how this ending is trying to make me feel. Should we be worried about Asgore's kidnapping? Susie doesn't seem very affected, and even his own child doesn't seem very shook up by it. That might or might not be because Kris is in on the plan, but Chapter 4's adventures through the Dark World only happened because Kris and Susie were trying to save Toriel from the Dark World. Asgore's name is said literally once after the kidnapping happens, and so I'm left with the feeling the game simply forgot that you hint at how people are meant to feel or what they should expect by having characters react to the events of the story.

![A screenshot of Deltarune Chapter 5, where Kris is walking through town early in the morning](/img/deltarune_hometown.png)
*If there's anything about Chapter 5 I would like to compliment, it's that I'm infernally jealous of how fantastic the lighting looks in this chapter, which greatly enhances the simple art style. It is beyond me how they accomplished it in Game Maker Studio out of all engines.*

And the writing quality in Chapter 5 is related to its most disappointing aspect: it reveals almost nothing. I've seen some arguments online made in Chapter 5's defense that fans are just disappointed their theories were proven wrong, but that's not the case when it's only revealed things the audience already knew or could reasonably guess. Asgore knows about the Dark Worlds? Hinted at before. The Dark World's game mechanics are in-universe? Hinted at before. Ralsei can use fire magic? Hinted at before. Rudy is dying? Hinted at before. Similarly, character beats like Ralsei becoming his own person, Susie wanting to keep the Dark World adventures going forever and Noelle visiting the Dark World aren't so much built upon as they are repeated. It even ends in almost the exact same way that Chapter 3 does, where the stakes are raised when the Knight kidnaps an adult character and we get another code to the shelter. 

So while the plot advanced in the literal sense, most of the plot's development happened either at the very beginning or very end of this chapter,with nothing consequential really happening between, but none of those plot points felt new or even that dramatic. I barely even mentioned the Plantners besides Flowery, because they're not that interesting or relevant to the story beyond serving as more eccentric enemies to fight. Let's just set aside the meta-narrative characters and questions surrounding them like the Forgotten Man and the Eggs, Onionsan, FRIEND and even Gaster and the Goners aside for the time being. They're not that important towards my enjoyment. Five chapters into a seven chapter story, we still don't have a single concrete answer to any of the following questions:
- Who is the Knight?
- What is the Knight's goal?
- What is Kris' goal?
- Who is Kris working with?
- What is Carol's goal?
- How did Asgore learn about the Dark Worlds?
- What were Asgore and Carol working together on?
- How did Dess go missing?
- How did Dess' disappearance affect Hometown?

This isn't nitpicking details that don't matter or are better kept vague; these are the most important questions the narrative itself raises and none of them have been answered so far even though we're supposedly over halfway through the game, even though the game had a perfectly good chance to explain a single one of these questions in a chapter that should've been about Asgore, yet doesn't really bother to explore much of him. Chapter 3 might have not shown us much about Toriel, but we at least got a whole bunch of clues about what the Dreemurr's homelife was like from Tenna, and even a few hints about what Dess was like. Meanwhile in Chapter 5, the Fun Gang interact with Asgore a mere *three times* in the Dark World, and only very briefly at that, meaning there's no time to actually learn *anything* from him. I understand *Deltarune* has a meta-narrative of having multiple characters deliberately deceive or hide information from the player, but then the story would need to be very engaging to keep people invested, and Chapter 5's story just isn't that interesting. 

It doesn't take a brilliant writer to recognize just how many wasted opportunities there are here, and all this in service of... more scenes with one-note flower characters? More platforming sections? *Undertale* also just dumped all of its backstory on the player in the final area of the game, which, while not ideal, is much more palatable considering it can be finished in one sitting. Why is *Deltarune*, an unfinished game that's already four times as long, repeating the same mistakes of dumping all of the answers in the final act of the game when it had much more time to build up its story? Why even have such a long chapter where important plot points like Susie and Noelle getting together or Asgore's character development are rushed through, but where the story moves forward by mere inches? Why did the team spend all this time and money on giving Flowery an animated introduction and using Hatsune Miku's official voicebanks for a song, but not on polishing up the writing more? 

I don't envy Toby's position; making a game on the scale of *Deltarune* must be hard, and it must be even harder to have to direct a team when you have very little experience with doing so, as the collaborations for *Undertale* mostly involved asking artists like Temmie to improve on his scribbles. And I'm sure he doesn't want to seem like the bad guy who throws out good work just because it's not exactly like how he pictured it in his head, but if you got a particular vision you want to share with the world then it's important you learn *how* to get your team on the same wavelength or you get something like *Deltarune Chapter 5*, which from a story standpoint mostly just repeats what happened before with a fresh coat of paint. It seems too insecure to stand on its own, having to rely on callbacks, flashy gimmicks and vibes to carry it when the writing, pacing, characters, and music aren't strong enough to carry the rest of the chapter anymore.

But in a story that's only seven chapters long, you can't afford to spin the wheel.
