Valve has been showing up a lot in gaming news recently. And not just regarding their recent and upcoming hardware like the Steam Deck's price increase, the Controller, Frame or Machine, but also the numerous lawsuits they've been facing, which are starting to become quite noticeable in their frequency. The most recent one, as of this writing, comes straight out of my own country and is [regarding Valve's "monopolistic behavior"](https://nltimes.nl/2026/06/11/dutch-gamers-file-eu220-million-claim-valve-operator-game-platform-steam) negatively affecting developers and consumers:

> A new Dutch class action has been filed against Valve Corporation, the company behind Steam. The case is brought by the Stichting Consumenten Competition Claims (CCC), which has launched the proceedings under the name GameClaim. The foundation acts on behalf of all Dutch PC gamers who have purchased games through Steam or other PC distribution platforms since 2013, IGN Benelux reports.
>
> \[...\]
>
> The foundation argues that Valve’s 30 percent cut on all game sales is overly high and reflects monopolistic behaviour.
>
> In alleged tying practices for in-game purchases, players must use the Steam Wallet to complete transactions, with Valve taking another 30 percent commission on those payments. Developers are also barred from referring players in-game to cheaper options available outside Steam.

The "30 percent cut" is one of the most common points brought up in arguments whether Valve, and by extension Steam, is dicking over consumers and developers with their alleged absurdly high cut. And indeed, Valve getting almost a third of your profits just for hosting a game on their store seems high on the surface, which isn't even taking taxes into account; depending on where you live you might need to pay taxes over your revenue twice with Valve having to pay taxes on a game's revenue before the developer even receives anything, but the whole story is more complex and messy than that. And really, that's a good summation of the entire situation. It's not nearly as simple as the people arguing against the cut (and Valve's position as a market leader) believe it is.

Let's address the elephant in the room first: does Valve hold any kind of monopoly? Having done some research on the matter before and after writing this article, as well as having read my share of _passionate_ debates on the subject, I can't find a consensus for this question, and I don't have enough expertise in economics to know for sure who to believe. At the very least, I _can_ say it isn't a monopoly in the strictest sense, as Steam does have its competitors — it simply is a better store than Impulse, Gamer's Gate, Games for Windows Live, the Windows Store, Twitch Game Store, the Discord Store and the Epic Games Store. You might not remember some of those if you don't follow the PC gaming market closely, which isn't surprising because [they are](https://www.escapistmagazine.com/games-for-windows-fail/), or were, [by and](https://www.escapistmagazine.com/dear-origin-you-stink/) large [terrible](https://www.shamusyoung.com/twentysidedtale/?p=8059). The only store actually trying to compete is GOG.com, which offers features that Steam doesn't such as DRM-free games and [the Preservation Program](https://www.gog.com/en/gog-preservation-program), at the cost of a smaller library of games. Some people might be fine with that, some might not and stick with their preferred platform, but it's the only digital store I've seen that tries to offer something that would at least tempt Steam users to use it non-begrudgingly. This in contrast to publishers like EA, Take-Two and Ubisoft, who simply force consumers to use their lackluster launchers to play games bought on Steam, like a sort of parasite. This actually ended up biting Take-Two in the ass when *Grand Theft Auto: The Trilogy - The Definitive Edition - Can This Name Get Longer* [accidentally shipped with cut content included in the files](https://www.pcgamer.com/dataminers-are-finding-developer-comments-and-unlicensed-songs-in-gta-trilogy-as-rockstar-games-launcher-remains-offline/), including commercial songs with expired licenses that were simply disabled in the code instead of being removed. As DE's files are handled by Rockstar's own launcher even if you bought the game on Steam, they had no choice but to shut the entire launcher down for days, making their games on PC unplayable, as they didn't have any tools to handle the situation. Steam does, because it's a mature platform used by millions of consumers and tens of thousands of developers.

As for whether the infamous Steam cut of 30% is too high or not, well, it heavily depends on who you ask. I've seen tons of independent developers who've only released games on PC rally against it as a uniquely greedy move from Valve, not realizing that's the standard take across most of the industry. Physical stores took 30% for physical games (and still do) and digital stores just followed suit. GOG.com, the Nintendo eShop, Microsoft Store, PlayStation Store and many more all take 30% as far as I know, but they do give developers plenty of handy features to make devs' lives much easier. Usually it's basic but handy stuff like achievements or leaderboards, but Steam in particular offers a lot of handy features like hosting forums, screenshots, artwork and  guides from users, and APIs for various things like controller support, multiplayer lobbies and so forth. The only notable exceptions I've seen are Itch.io, which is unsurprisingly rather barebones in comparison to any the aforementioned stores due to its completely optional cut, and the Epic Games Store, which touted its 12% cut at launch as if it were a huge benefit for developers and consumers. In the vast majority of cases, the publishers would just pocket that difference instead of trickling savings down to consumers. So while most games were priced exactly the same, it's the sentiment that counts, right? They did announce just last year [that developers wouldn't have to pay anything on their first million dollars of revenue per app per year](https://store.epicgames.com/news/new-epic-games-store-webshops-and-revenue-share-update?lang=en-US), which seems incredibly generous for indie devs on the surface, but then Epic Games isn't making huge bank off those games anyway and is desperate for some good PR to get people to use their store for anything but claiming free games. Considering I only found out about this while doing research for this article, I don't think it worked. According to their own documents, the company lost _273 million dollars_ on the store in 2020 and that was back when Fortnite alone could keep the whole ship afloat. Thinking what the situation looks like for Epic now, with Fortnite gradually losing its cultural relevance and their store still considered an afterthought by most, those low cuts don't seem sustainable.

Some people might point out at this point that, depending on the amount of revenue a game makes on Steam, Valve only takes 25% or even 20%, but then this is slightly missing the point. It's true, but that's because those games make enough profit for Valve to singlehandedly keep the platform afloat even with that lower cut. As an indie developer of practically no renown, I wouldn't be surprised if Valve told me they're losing money on every single game I've worked on available on their platform. And if you're reading this, there's at least a chance you've at least heard of [_Paperball_](./designpaperball) or [_Salaryman Shi_](./pixelartsalarymanshi); imagine the thousands of indie games on the platform that have less than 5 reviews and almost nobody's ever played. Hosting and moving all that data costs a ton of money when your platform moves [100 exabytes of Internet traffic a year](https://store.steampowered.com/news/group/4145017/view/528746884222682052), which is somewhere around __1.14% of the entirety of yearly Internet traffic__. That shit don't cost a dollar, especially when [you need to work out deals with ISPs across the world](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wrvr02SiHY4&t=1408s) just to have the guarantee the store won't stop existing simply from the insane amounts of HTTP requests it gets every minute, particularly around the time of the Winter and Summer Sale. With that context in mind, the fact Valve allows us to put up our unprofitable art for a mere hundred dollars per product plus 30% of revenue isn't too bad. If you want to see what it'd look like for indie devs if they're stuck on a platform owned by a truly greedy company, Google kicked all of Cliax Games' games off Google Play a few years back because they demanded specific legal documents from us but rejected the ones we sent in without actually telling us what was wrong with them, and then never replied to any of our e-mails. They still keep sending us e-mails to inform us of legal changes to accommodate governments of countries I can't even pinpoint on a map, though. _That's_ what an anti-developer monopoly looks like; if your games don't make us the big bucks, you can get the big fucked.

As for the entire "Valve doesn't allow you to sell games outside of Steam for cheaper" point, there's a lot of misinformation going around about that online. As someone who somewhat knows what he's talking about, the situation is actually pretty simple and understandable. Valve is typically pretty hands-off about pricing on other platforms, with the exception of Steam keys. You can request batches of Steam keys and sell them without having to pay Valve their cut, but the only thing they will explicitly not tolerate is if you give the people who buy Steam keys a better deal than Steam customers, which honestly seems pretty understandable to me. I can imagine they had to draw the line here when certain developers kept taking advantage of it, because if you're making money off your game on Steam that makes use of Steam's features while Valve and Steam customers are getting a raw deal, that's called abusing the situation. It's hard to say it's a morally dubious or even illegal monopoly when that's the only real hard line the company draws. Still, nothing's stopping you from selling those Steam keys on your own store as long as you don't screw over customers too hard, though having made and hosted a few webshops in my time and remembering what a pain in the butt it is... You can't just slap some keys on a default WordPress with WooCommerce installation and expect people would want to use that when they're used to much better. That's not even getting in all the requests you're going to get from bots, script kiddies, bots from script kiddies, dealing with heavy traffic and/or potential DDoS attacks and... Yeah, Gabe can keep his 30%.

Now from how much I'm defending Valve here, you might assume I'm a huge Valve and/or Steam fanboy. I wouldn't blame anyone for coming to that conclusion, but if I were to detail my gripes with Valve and Steam as either a consumer or developer, this article would be twice as long and it's already a damn big collection of words as is. Obviously I would love a larger share of revenue, but I also know it's not feasible under these specific circumstances. Valve's already the closest thing we've got to hippies in this industry, but even hippies need to make money somehow, and God knows they forgot how to make games. Most gripes regarding Valve, Steam and their position as King of Digital Stores seem to be less about what the company's doing wrong and more like people don't like the fact there's a monopoly at all. That's understandable in the sense that some competition is good for everyone involved, but so far everyone who has tried simply could not comprehend how Steam acquired its natural monopoly in the first place. They think if you're a big company that builds a platform at all, [the people will come eventually](https://arch-img.b4k.dev/v/1774403290884.jpg), which isn't how it works if you show up years later to the party with a subpar store. And at the end of the day, if there's any company in this industry I would trust a monopoly with, it's Valve. They've pulled their shares of boners in the past like [the whole paid mods fiasco](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/5-takeaways-from-gabe-newell-s-reddit-q-a-on-paid-mods), but were smart enough to drop that after the huge controversy it caused. Compare that to Bethesda, who [didn't let a little backlash stop them](https://fallout.bethesda.net/en/atomic-shop) and happily [torched the last bit of goodwill](https://creations.bethesda.net/en/starfield/all) they still had for a few bucks. A company backing down after a bad PR move is a rare event in this industry, but it shows Valve is at least willing to get their head out of their ass if people tell them to. It's sad that's how low the bar is, but imagine if it were anyone else.

If PC gaming were in the hands of Microsoft, EA or Amazon, I don't think we'd have an industry left right now.
